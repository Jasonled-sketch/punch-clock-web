## 1. Table types — which suits light square-tube fabrication (stands, frames, brackets)?

### Takeaway
No source gives a head-to-head ranking, but the builds and forum advice converge: for a budget shop making tube frames, the two realistic DIY choices are (a) a plain 10–12 mm plate on a stiff tube frame with generous overhang, or (b) a slatted top of thick flat bars bolted/shimmed onto a tube frame with ~45–50 mm clamp gaps; hole-grid fixture tops only pay off if the plate is ≥12 mm, flat to begin with, and the holes are on a true 50 mm (2 in) grid — which in practice means laser/waterjet-cut or bought. Tab-and-slot kits and cast blocks give the best "bought" flatness per dollar but are imports; I-beam tops are the cheap route to long, very stiff tables when scrap beams are available.

### Cited Findings

**(1) Solid plate on tube frame**
- A solid plate is described as the simpler DIY path, with "excellent thermal mass" and easy cleaning, versus a flat-bar grid — [coohom guide (low-quality content site)](https://www.coohom.com/article/diy-weld-table-build-your-perfect-welding-surface-at-home)
- One builder bought a 4 ft × 8 ft × 3/4 in (1.22 × 2.44 m × 19 mm) plate that "came remarkably flat from the supplier" and skipped grinding it because his work did not need more — [Garage Journal, "Welding table build, fixture, jig, flat? Need advice."](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/welding-table-build-fixture-jig-flat-need-advice.332209/) (thread attribution approximate; excerpt came from a Garage Journal result set)
- "Most of the new 1/2 plate I have encountered is dead flat from the warehouse. 1/2" plate with a bow in it is tough to straighten as it is probably stretched." — [TractorByNet, "Welding table build"](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3642870)
- Forum posters note that "a lot of welding tables out there aren't as perfectly flat as the owners think" and that a sheet-topped table is best for general fabrication, not precision work — [Hobart Weld Talk, "Help with welding table"](https://weldtalk.hobartwelders.com/forum/weld-talk-topic-archive/welding-processes/18600-help-with-welding-table); [Welding Tips and Tricks forum, "Welding table Flatness"](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=14342)

**(2) Fixture table with hole grid (16 mm / 5/8 in or 28 mm)**
- Fireball Tool's "Avoid These Mistakes When You Build A Fixture Welding Table": (i) do not start from warped plate — "Straightening a bent piece of heavy plate steel is very difficult" and the distortion "will transfer into everything" built on it; use bent plate for a plain workbench instead; (ii) 1/2 in (12.7 mm) "is a minimum thickness plate if you want to avoid problems"; (iii) "I see lots of DIY tables with 5/8 holes with a 3, 4, and 6in hole centers" — too far apart for off-the-shelf tooling and hard to map in CAD; tee slots are less accurate than a hole grid; (iv) fixed height is a mistake — use adjustable legs if possible — [Fireball Forum](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/avoid-these-mistakes-when-you-build-a-fixture-welding-table/2902). (Fireball sells fixture tables — vendor bias.)
- Typical grid: 5/8 in or 16 mm holes on exactly 2 in (50.8 mm) centers; 28 mm and 16 mm are the two common systems — [metalworkinghub](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-does-a-welding-table-work/); [Kaijian vendor guide](https://www.kjweldingtable.com/weld-fixture-table-buying-guide-types-features-and-how-to-choose-the-right-one)
- Hole accuracy is make-or-break: a Fireball forum poster describes a drilling-jig approach and a co-worker who thought "1/16 is close enough" (1.6 mm) and ruined an otherwise accurate table; another DIY table used 3/4 in (19 mm) plate with waterjet holes on mixed 2 in and 4 in spacing — [Fireball Forum, "DIY fixture table"](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/diy-fixture-table/2643); [Fireball Forum, "What i THINK i've learned while planning my fixture plate"](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/what-i-think-ive-learned-while-planning-my-fixture-plate/2458)
- Counter-view: "hundreds of holes can jeopardize the strength of the top plate" and leave too little metal to absorb weld heat; "a small table needs only maybe 4 holes" — [HomemadeTools forum, "Welding table"](https://www.homemadetools.net/forum/welding-table-95112-post217970); [TractorByNet, "Welding table build" (386590)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.386590/)
- Low-density hole patterns seen on DIY plate tables: 5/8 in holes on 12 in (305 mm) centers; on 6 in (152 mm) centers; a separate sub-plate with 300 tapped 5/8-11 holes on 1.5 in centers; 5/8 in chosen so StrongHand drop-in clamps fit — [TractorByNet (386590)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.386590/)
- Slots cut into a plate worried one forum poster: "its going to stress relieve and warp" — [WeldingWeb, "Welding table question: slots for clamps – what size?"](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/630851-Welding-table-question-slots-for-clamps-what-size)
- One guide's builder drilled 5/8 in holes on 2 in centers only **after** the top had been made flat — [metalworkinghub, "How to Make a Welding Jig Table"](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-to-make-a-welding-jig-table/)
- Tooling cost caveat: "a functional modular clamping kit for a 2×4 table easily adds $800–$1,500" — [Kaijian vendor guide](https://www.kjweldingtable.com/welding-table-jigs-how-to-choose-and-use-them-for-precise-metalwork)

**(3) Slatted / "picket" top (flat bars or tubes with clamp gaps)**
- Practical Machinist "Standard duty welding table: critique my plan": frame of 3 in square tube, 3/16 in wall (76 × 76 × 4.8 mm); table ≈ 2 ft 4 in × 4 ft (0.71 × 1.22 m), ≈ 750 lb (340 kg); top of 1018 cold-rolled bars 5 in and 6 in × 3/4 in (127/152 × 19 mm) with 1.75 in (44 mm) spaces; the builder planned a hole pattern in the frame beams so slats can be re-spaced from gapped to nearly solid — [Practical Machinist](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/standard-duty-welding-table-critique-my-plan.302352/)
- Slat-table advocates on Practical Machinist: 4 in (100 mm) slats with ~2 in (50 mm) gaps, "bolts are the answer for slat tables"; slats "can be shimmed to make the surface flat" / "the plates are infinitely shim-able into dead flat"; "square and rectangular tubing has been pretty darn flat on the faces" — [Practical Machinist, "Welding table design"](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/welding-table-design.274951/); [Practical Machinist, "I'm building my own welding table"](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/im-building-my-own-welding-table-looking-for-advice-and-suggestions.355815/) (which of these threads each quote is from could not be confirmed)
- TractorByNet slat build: frame ≈ 36 × 75.5 in (0.91 × 1.92 m); legs and top frame 2 × 4 × 1/4 in rectangular tube (50 × 100 × 6.4 mm); lower braces 2 × 2 × 1/4 in (50 × 50 × 6.4 mm); slats 6 × 5/8 in flat bar (152 × 16 mm) spaced 1.75 in (44 mm). Regret: he "would have mounted the slats to the top frame first, then laid out the hole locations" — [TractorByNet (386590)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.386590/)
- Garage Journal "Jim's welding table build": slat top of 1 × 4 × 36 in cold-rolled bars (25 × 102 × 914 mm) on C3×5 channel (76 mm channel); slats held from below (threaded rod / bolts welded underneath) so the top face has no holes; another builder drilled blind holes in the slat undersides and screwed upward through the support tube — [Garage Journal](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/jims-welding-table-build.381589/post-6977727)
- Gap width matters: a builder found that with only a 1 in (25 mm) gap between slats, fitting clamps "all went to hell" — [Pelican Parts forum, "Building a welding table"](https://forums.pelicanparts.com/porsche-911-technical-forum/1029323-building-welding-table.html) (attribution between this and the Garage Journal thread above is uncertain). The same Pelican thread reports a 5/16 in (8 mm) slat top was "plenty strong enough for light duty garage type stuff".
- Garage Journal 2 ft × 4 ft × 36 in (0.61 × 1.22 × 0.91 m) slat recipe: eight 6 in × 1/2 in hot-rolled bars 30 in long (152 × 12.7 × 762 mm; 20 ft / 6.1 m of bar total) on 2 × 2 × 0.120 in tube (50 × 50 × 3 mm) — [Garage Journal, "This could be the ultimate welding table"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/this-could-be-the-ultimate-welding-table.180145/post-6103582)
- Cost claim: "flat bar is as cheap or cheaper than solid plate" — [Garage Journal, "Welding fixture table types to fabricate"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/welding-fixture-table-types-to-fabricate.543903/post-11081387) (anecdotal; local steel prices decide)
- A "Heavy Duty Welding Table" Instructable uses 4 in × 1/2 in × 40 in flat-bar slats (102 × 12.7 × 1016 mm) on C-channel cross members with 5 in schedule-40 pipe legs — [Instructables mirror](https://structables.lunar.icu/Heavy-Duty-Welding-Table)
- Budget variant: 2 in × 1/4 in flat bars (50 × 6 mm) spaced 1 in (25 mm) — [welders-supply.com DIY plans](https://welders-supply.com/welding-equipment/welding-tables/diy-welding-table-plans/) (commercial blog; note the 25 mm gap conflicts with the clamp-fit complaint above)

**(4) Torsion-box / tab-and-slot kits and cast blocks**
- CertiFlat (weldtables.com) FabBlock/Pro Top kits: retailer listings cite ±0.003 in (±0.076 mm) manufacturing tolerance on parts and "engineered tab and slot stiffening ribs for a guaranteed flat surface"; the OctoBlock is listed at ±0.015 in (±0.38 mm) flatness — [Summit Racing listing](https://www.summitracing.com/search/product-line/weldtables-certiflat-heavy-duty-pro-top-kits); [reach.dog listing](https://reach.dog/shop/weldtablescom/products/certiflat-fb2436-fabblock-u-weld-kit-modular-welding-table). The ±0.003 in is a part-cutting tolerance from retailer copy, not a measured as-welded flatness.
- Hot Bike magazine build of a CertiFlat tab-and-slot table: parts were tight enough to need a mallet; the design "only goes together one way as it squares itself up"; clamps were still used so parts didn't wander; top tacked via tabs then welded — [Hot Bike, "How to Build a Tab and Slot Welding Table"](https://hotbike.com/?p=6534)
- A Practical Machinist user reports a 24 × 48 in (0.61 × 1.22 m) FabBlock in 3/8 in (9.5 mm) plate with alternating 16 mm holes and tapped 1/2-13 holes on 2 in centers "fit well" — [Practical Machinist, "Certiflat fab block table"](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/certiflat-fab-block-table.380992/)
- Texas Metal Works (Weld Tables) 50 × 80 in (1.27 × 2.03 m) rib-style top kit, offered in 1/4 in (6.4 mm) and 3/8 in (9.5 mm): tack each rib at two points, check square by measuring diagonals before full beads, skip-weld to limit heat, let cool slowly, then check with a straightedge and "correct minor distortion with strategic tack welds on the underside" — [Texas Metal Works product page](https://texasmetalworks.com/product/50x80-welding-table-top)
- Langmuir ARCFLAT is not a tab-and-slot kit: it is a 24 × 36 in (610 × 914 mm) machined cast-iron block with 336 holes, published flatness ±0.0075 in (±0.19 mm), square to 0.1°, and blocks bolt together to make larger tables — [Langmuir Systems](https://langmuirsystems.com/arcflat). One owner measured ≈0.054 in (1.4 mm) out and blamed "very thin [castings] with small ribs" — [Fireball Forum review](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/langmuir-arcflat-table-disappointment-review/2121) (single anecdote, posted on a competitor's forum)
- Fireball "Pro Welding Fixture Table Kit" sizes: 36 × 24, 54 × 30, 54 × 54 and 72 × 36 in, all 1/2 in plate (e.g. 1829 × 914 × 12.7 mm); plates "ground flat, with machined holes, chamfered on both sides"; hardware includes "frame straps to secure the 1x2 [25 × 50 mm tube] to the plate without welding", clips fitted loosely then locked with grub screws — [Fireball Tool product page](https://www.fireballtool.com/products/pro-table-kit-3-x-2-72-x-36-x); [Fireball Forum, "New DIY fixture tables"](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/new-diy-fixture-tables/2984); [Fireball Forum, "DIY Fixture Table Kit Assembly"](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/diy-fixture-table-kit-assembly/3110)
- Even bought tables disappoint: a Garage Journal buyer reported Fireball table plates about 0.018 in (0.46 mm) low — [Garage Journal, "Fireball table – NOT impressed"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/fireball-table-not-impressed.529289/)

**(5) I-beam / channel tables**

### Inferences
- **Best fit for this user (inference, not a sourced verdict):** square-tube stands and frames need (a) two long straight reference edges and a known-square corner, (b) clamp access all round and through the middle, (c) coplanar support under four corners of a frame. A slatted top of 100–150 × 12–16 mm flat bars across a 2-rail tube frame with ~45–50 mm gaps gives (b) cheaply with only a chop saw and drill, each bar can be shimmed individually (so the frame need not be perfect), and bars are replaceable. A single 10–12 mm plate gives a better continuous reference surface and spatter-free cleanup but needs a crane-less way to handle a 100–200 kg sheet and offers clamping only at the edges unless holes are added.
- A hybrid seen across builds — plate with a sparse pattern of 16 mm holes, or slats that can be pushed together — captures most of the benefit without the cost of a true 50 mm grid.
- A true 16 mm / 50 mm fixture grid is only worth it if the top is laser-cut (as in the Taiwan Fineworks and Fractory builds in section 8); hand-drilled grids lose the accuracy that justifies the tooling.
- Two shorter tables (e.g. 2 × ~0.9–1.2 m) levelled to each other approximate one 1.8–2.4 m table, halve the plate handling weight and suit a small shop, but the sources stress that getting two tops co-planar is the hard part — adjustable feet on every leg are then mandatory.
- Weight check (my arithmetic, steel 7.85 kg/m²·mm): 10 mm plate ≈ 78.5 kg/m² → 0.75 × 1.8 m ≈ 106 kg; 0.9 × 2.4 m ≈ 170 kg; 12 mm → ≈ 127 kg and ≈ 203 kg respectively.

## 2. Frame design — tube sizes, legs, cross-member spacing, bracing, load

### Takeaway
Documented builds cluster around 50 × 50 × 3–5 mm tube for small tables and 63–76 mm × 4.8 mm (or 50 × 100 rectangular on edge) for 2 m+ or heavy-top tables, with cross-members every ~300–400 mm under a ≤12 mm top and a clamping overhang of 50–150 mm; long 4-leg tables sag measurably over time, so 6 legs (or a deep apron) are advised from ~2.4 m.

### Cited Findings
- 4 × 10 ft × 1/2 in top (1.22 × 3.05 m × 12.7 mm, $400.77) on 80 ft of 2.5 in × 3/16 in tube (63.5 × 4.8 mm, $284.53), "simple ladder type construction" for the base — [TractorByNet, "Welding table build"](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/) (prices are as posted, year not captured)
- Cross-supports are the main fix for sag: three angle-iron supports across the 4 ft (1.22 m) direction; "angle under the top about every 16 in [406 mm]" prevents sag and stops the top bouncing when hammered — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3642870)
- Guides: cross-members "every 12 to 16 inches" (305–406 mm) under the top; another says 2–3 cross-members 18–24 in (457–610 mm) apart — [builtwithtools.com](https://builtwithtools.com/how-to-make-a-welding-table/); [metalworkinghub.com](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-to-make-a-welding-table/) (which guide gave which figure is not certain; both are content sites)
- Perimeter/cross tube recommendations: 2 × 2 × 1/4 in (50 × 50 × 6.4 mm) or 2 × 3 in (50 × 75 mm) rectangular; "for tables 6'+ [1.8 m+], upgrade to 3 in [76 mm] sections or add diagonal bracing" — [metalworkinghub.com](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-to-make-a-welding-table/)
- 2 × 2 in tube in 11 gauge (≈3 mm) or 3/16 in (4.8 mm) wall is the common small-table spec; xTool's guide uses 2 × 2 × 0.120 in (50 × 50 × 3 mm) with a 1/4 in (6.4 mm) top, 3/8 in (9.5 mm) for heavier use — [xTool Academy](https://www.xtool.com/blogs/xtool-academy/diy-welding-table); [welders-supply.com](https://welders-supply.com/welding-equipment/welding-tables/diy-welding-table-plans/)
- Forum opinion that 2 × 2 × 3/16 in HSS (50 × 50 × 4.8 mm) legs are a sensible choice; another build used 3 × 3 in legs with 2 × 2 in stretchers; a 4 × 8 ft builder planned six legs — [WeldingWeb, "What size legs should I put on my welding table?"](https://www.weldingweb.com/threads/what-size-legs-should-i-put-on-my-welding-table.327331/); [Garage Journal, "Suggestions for welding table legs"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/suggestions-for-welding-table-legs.393133/)
- Long-table regret: a builder who omitted centre legs says the table "has developed a 1/8 in sway over the 10 ft length" (≈3 mm over 3.05 m) — [Practical Machinist welding-table threads](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/standard-duty-welding-table-critique-my-plan.302352/) (exact thread uncertain)
- Support spacing matters as much as thickness: "even 1 inch [25 mm] plate can flex when sitting on 4 pads" about 7 ft (2.1 m) apart — [TractorByNet, "Welding Table"](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table.204056/post-2813451)
- Top-thickness opinions for big tops: a 3/8 in (9.5 mm) top on ~4 × 8 ft over a 2 × 2 frame "isn't very stout given the distance you are spanning"; suggested minimum 5/8 in (16 mm) for that size — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3654731). Contradicted in practice by the DTN farm table (3/8 in on 2 × 2 tube; "a 3-pound sledge did not move the table") — [DTN/Progressive Farmer](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2021/10/17/diy-welding-table-portable-takes)
- "1/2 inch minimum as I have experienced issues"; another user who moved up to a 3/8 in surface called it "a huge improvement" — [IH8MUD, "Welding Table"](https://forum.ih8mud.com/threads/welding-table.824324/page-2)
- 1/4 in (6 mm) tops "oil-can" under concentrated heat; 3/8 in is "a practical middle ground"; 1/2 in for very low deflection and heavy use — [metalworkinghub.com](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-to-make-a-welding-table/)
- Overhang: "~6 in [150 mm] around the perimeter of the top to enable you to clamp things" — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3654731); other builds planned 2–3 in (50–75 mm) all round — [TractorByNet, "Building a Shop / Weld Table"](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-10)
- Japanese Q&A answers: use square tube, not angle, for legs (angle legs wobble); examples given are 100 × 100 × 4.5 mm legs 900 mm long ×4, and a 6 mm × 4×8-shaku (1219 × 2438 mm) top on a 150 × 150 × 4.5 mm tube frame with ribs at 600 mm pitch; a 6 mm top should get a welded grid of 30 × 30 mm tube under it; a 1400 × 750 × 750 mm table is heavy and shakes if frame stiffness is lacking; 9 mm considered the baseline top (800 × 600 mm × 9 mm "minimum") — [Yahoo!知恵袋](https://detail.chiebukuro.yahoo.co.jp/qa/question_detail/q11252037071); [Yahoo!不動産 知恵袋](https://realestate.yahoo.co.jp/knowledge/chiebukuro/detail/14201948514/)
- Japanese guide: 5–6 mm top is OK for light/tack work, 9–12 mm if hammering — [trunktrunk.click guide](https://trunktrunk.click/yousetsudaiwojiukurikatakanzengaido.html) (anonymous content site)
- Fractory customer build: frame "designed specifically for rigidity and strength" from 60 × 40 × 4 mm tube, laser-cut plates, 16 mm holes at 50 mm — [Fractory](https://fractory.com/making-a-diy-welding-table/) (vendor-sponsored)
- Casters, as they appear in builds: a 1/2 in plate top "will overload most 3 in casters before the table gets very big"; load-rated casters add at least 3 in (76 mm) to height; bolt rather than weld casters so they can be replaced; one rule is total caster capacity ≥ 2 × (table weight + heaviest workpiece) — [TractorByNet (392188)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-10); [Pro-Touring, "Welding table construction advice needed"](https://www.pro-touring.com/threads/94427-Welding-table-construction-advice-needed) (per-quote attribution uncertain)

### Inferences
- **My deflection estimates (simply-supported beam formulas, E = 200 GPa; conservative because welded joints add fixity):**
  - *Top plate between cross-members*, treating a 300 mm-wide strip as a beam under a 100 kg point load at mid-span: 10 mm plate → ≈0.27 mm at 400 mm spacing, ≈0.9 mm at 600 mm; 12 mm plate → ≈0.15 mm / ≈0.5 mm; 6 mm plate → ≈1.2 mm at 400 mm. Deflection scales with span³ and 1/thickness³, which is why the forum "every 16 in / ~400 mm" rule and "10–12 mm" tops go together.
  - *Long apron rails* (second moment ≈ 21 cm⁴ for 50 × 50 × 3; ≈ 105 cm⁴ for 75 × 75 × 4.5; ≈ 112 cm⁴ for 100 × 50 × 3 on edge): with 4 legs on a 2.4 m table (≈2.2 m clear span) a 100 kg mid-span load deflects a 50 × 50 × 3 rail ≈5 mm, and the 10 mm top's own weight (~35 kg/m per rail) adds ≈2.5 mm — unacceptable. The same rail with a centre pair of legs (≈1.1 m span) gives ≈0.7 mm and ≈0.16 mm. A 100 × 50 × 3 on edge or 75 × 75 × 4.5 rail on 4 legs gives ≈1 mm under the point load.
  - Conclusion: at 1.8–2.4 m either use 6 legs with 50 × 50 rails, or 4 legs with ≥100 mm-deep rails (100 × 50 × 3 on edge / 75 × 75 × 4.5); 6 legs on an uneven floor need 6 adjustable feet, whereas 4 legs + deep rails are easier to level — a real trade-off for the user's uneven concrete floor.
- Strength is not the limit — stiffness is. Any of these frames carries far more than hobby loads; published "capacity" figures are meaningless without a deflection criterion.
- Racking: none of the excerpts quantify diagonal bracing; builds rely on a welded lower stretcher/shelf frame plus fully welded leg joints. For a table on casters that gets pushed around, a lower stretcher ring at ~150–200 mm above floor is the common pattern (seen in the slat build, DTN build, and guide "H-frame" descriptions).

### Gaps
- No source found gives a tested load rating or engineering deflection rule for DIY frames; the numbers above are my own beam calculations.
- No explicit guidance found on diagonal bracing sizes; only a generic "upgrade to 76 mm sections or add diagonal bracing for 1.8 m+" line.
- The exact Practical Machinist thread containing the "1/8 in over 10 ft" sag regret was not confirmed.

## 3. Attaching the top without warping; checking flatness; what flatness is realistic

### Takeaway
The consensus is: finish and true the frame first, never seam-weld the top, and either bolt/shim the top (adjustable, zero heat) or hold it with a few short, scattered stitches (25–50 mm every 150–300 mm, alternating sides); realistic DIY flatness is about 0.4–0.8 mm (1/64–1/32 in) over the table, which forum users consider sufficient for general frame fabrication.

### Cited Findings
- "Don't completely fabricate the frame and then weld it to the plate because that will just pull the plate to the shape of the fabrication"; instead "put a straightedge across the frame, if it's not flat then add shims between frame/top and clamp the top down with U-bolts or clamps"; or fasten with countersunk bolts and shim high spots — [Welding Tips and Tricks forum, "Welding Table advice"](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=15622)
- Bolting options described in the Garage Journal "Attaching welding table top" thread: drill and tap the top, or countersunk screws into angle/channel inside the frame so the top is removable and gets no weld heat; one user bolted the top down with "a heavy duty O-ring between the top and frame at every bolt" and adjusted until very flat; another welded nuts under the frame so the bolts double as levelling jacks — [Garage Journal](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/attaching-welding-table-top.418271/post-7804775)
- Stitch patterns quoted: "about 1 1/2 inches every foot or so" (38 mm per 305 mm) around the outside of the frame; "not run welds longer than 1 in every foot or so" (25 mm per 305 mm); "two-inch beads every six inches, jumping around" (50 mm per 150 mm) — [TractorByNet, "Welding Table: My first big project"](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-my-first-big-project.237944/post-2766106); [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3660325); [Miller forum, "Welding Table – Flattening Top"](https://forum.millerwelds.com/forum/welding-discussions/7508-welding-table-flattening-top/page3) (matching of each quote to thread is approximate)
- Over-welding the underside leaves "little depressions or bows all over the table"; scattered light tacks are enough — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3660325)
- Worked examples from the 4 × 10 ft × 12.7 mm build thread: (a) flatten the base, pull the plate tight onto it, stitch with short widely spaced welds — "not perfectly flat but close enough for anything I'd ever need"; (b) lay the plate bow-down, clamp the perimeter to the frame, then stitch in a grid, alternating sides of the tubes. A poster warns the plate may spring back to its bow when straps/clamps come off, so the frame must be stiff enough to hold it — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/)
- "Any welding or cutting a plate like that could introduce some bow due to stress reliefs in the plate" — finish the frame before mounting the top — [Garage Journal, "Advise on building weld/fab table using 1 in plate"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/advise-on-building-weld-fab-table-using-1-plate.428521/)
- Shim method result: check flatness, make shims to the measured gaps, hold with "lots of small stitches or tacks"; a builder reports about 1/64–1/32 in (0.4–0.8 mm) flatness this way — [Garage Journal (428521)](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/advise-on-building-weld-fab-table-using-1-plate.428521/); [Practical Machinist, "How to flatten a fab table top"](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/how-to-flatten-a-fab-table-top.216849/) (source of the figure is one of these two)
- Welding legs directly to a top with no frame is "a good way to warp the table permanently"; build the whole frame before welding the top to it — [Everlast forum](https://www.everlastgenerators.com/node/869)
- Extreme How-To build article (Blair Weilnau): tack the frame, level the legs, add flat-steel cross supports, grind frame-top welds flush before fitting the 1/4 in (6.4 mm) plate; continuous welds warp the table, so skip-weld with gaps — [Extreme How-To](https://extremehowto.com/how-to-build-a-welding-table/)
- Long-term drift: a 3/8 in (9.5 mm) plate welded to its frame was flat after cooling but years later had gone out of flat in its most-used area; fix suggested = steel shims (washers work) and, if precision matters, a thin sacrificial sheet on top — [Welding Tips and Tricks forum, "Welding table Flatness"](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=14342)
- Rescuing a warped top: heat a spot on the underside to cherry red starting at the worst point, with a hose to quench; light hammering along weld lines, checking often; warning that welding top to frame "can string a bow into the top"; easier to make the frame hold the plate flat (countersunk bolts) than to flatten the plate; last resort is machining — one shop had a welded 3/4 in (19 mm) table machined then ground — [Garage Journal, "I warped my welding table top"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/i-warped-my-welding-table-top-please-advise-re-correction-procedures.377606/); [WeldingWeb, "Welding Table Top Flattening?"](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/567701-Welding-Table-Top-Flattening); [Practical Machinist (216849)](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/how-to-flatten-a-fab-table-top.216849/)
- How flat is "flat enough": Fireball forum answers — "Flatness should be 1/32 in [0.8 mm] or flatter end to end", and "as flat as its context requires" — [Fireball Forum, "How flat should a welding table be?"](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/how-flat-should-a-welding-table-be/2687)
- Benchmarks: a Practical Machinist shop standard of 0.005 in per foot (≈0.42 mm/m) for automation tabletops; a 6 ft (1.83 m) table flat to <0.015 in (0.38 mm); professional modular tables ≤0.15 mm over the whole top; McMaster "ultra-flat" tables 0.002 in/ft (≈0.17 mm/m) — [Practical Machinist](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/standard-duty-welding-table-critique-my-plan.302352/); [GPPH](https://gpph-group.com/en/?p=35711); [McMaster-Carr](https://www.mcmaster.com/products/ultra-flat-tables)
- Texas Metal Works FAQ: there is "no single tolerance for flatness"; flatness = maximum deviation of any point from a mid-plane; a table can leave the factory at 0.004 in (0.10 mm), get strapped to a pallet and forklifted, and arrive with 0.020 in (0.5 mm) of twist — [Texas Metal Works FAQ](https://texasmetalworks.com/faq/how-flat-should-a-welding-table-be-and-what-tolerance-is-acceptable)
- Checking tools named in threads: straightedge across the frame/top; winding sticks; water level; laser line projected above the frame and measuring down; 4 ft level with shims — [WeldingWeb (376541)](https://www.weldingweb.com/threads/how-to-create-a-flat-table-without-a-flat-surface.376541/); [WTAT forum (15622)](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=15622); [WeldingWeb I-beam table](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/archive/index.php/t-539631.html)

### Inferences
- For this user the lowest-risk method is: frame first → true the top plane of the frame (shim pads, not grinding the tubes) → rest the top on it → fasten with bolts from below into drilled/tapped holes or welded-on tabs, with shim washers at each bolt. That gives zero heat input and lets the top be re-shimmed after the frame settles or the table is moved to a different patch of floor (twist from the floor is real — see the Texas Metal Works pallet remark).
- If welding is preferred for cost/speed: ≤25–40 mm stitches at ~300 mm pitch on the underside frame-to-plate joint, staggered left/right and done in a skipping order, is within every quoted pattern. With a 250 A MIG it is easy to put in too much heat — run short and cool.
- A slatted top sidesteps most of this: each bar is bolted or lightly stitched at two or three points and shimmed individually.
- A 0.5–1 mm overall flatness target, checked with a 1–2 m straightedge plus feeler gauges on both diagonals (twist) and with winding sticks, is realistic and matches the forum "1/32 in" benchmark; chasing 0.15 mm (commercial modular-table class) is not realistic for a hand-built welded table.
- A removable top also allows flipping the plate when one face gets spatter-pitted — implied by the bolted-top advocates, though no excerpt states it outright.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how much a given stitch pattern distorts a 10–12 mm top; all numbers are builder anecdotes.
- No excerpt described "locating tabs only / top just resting" in detail, although removable bolted tops were common.
- Machinist-level (0.02 mm/m) use for table checking was not specifically documented in the retrieved excerpts.

## 4. Bootstrapping — building a flat, square, co-planar frame with no flat table

### Takeaway
Builders either (a) use the top plate itself as the reference (level/shim the plate, build the frame onto it upside-down), or (b) build the frame on levelled stands and bring members into one plane with winding sticks, a laser/water level and shims — then rely on adjustable feet and a shim-able top to remove what is left.

### Cited Findings
- WeldingWeb "How to create a flat table without a flat surface?": most-recommended option is to get the plate level/flat first and build the frame onto it; shim the sheet flat on plywood/particle board; "use winding sticks to get the frame elements in the same plane… a water level would help… final level would be achieved with leveler in the legs"; frame must be strong enough to hold the sheet flat since a half-ton sheet is unlikely to be perfectly flat; threaded-rod adjustable legs so the table can be moved and re-levelled — [WeldingWeb](https://www.weldingweb.com/threads/how-to-create-a-flat-table-without-a-flat-surface.376541/); [WeldingWeb (vbb mirror)](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/376541-How-to-create-a-flat-table-without-a-flat-surface?p=3951661)
- "For something that size, I would use a gravity horizontal laser line projected over the top of the frame and measure from the line down to the frame to adjust the frame for level"; threaded rod and nuts between the top frame and legs to make the top adjustable — [WTAT forum, "Welding Table advice"](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=15622)
- Build on a true, level set-up of pipe jacks, or flip the work so the top sits level — [WeldingWeb, "No flat surfaces — where to weld?"](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/20224-No-flat-surfaces-where-to-weld?p=174098)
- I-beam table sequence: set the leg pads on the floor first, cut each leg to its measured height, shim the beams level with a 4 ft level, hold with many large tacks — [WeldingWeb, "I-beam welding table"](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/archive/index.php/t-539631.html)
- Kit-maker sequence that transfers to DIY: tack at two points per member, check square by measuring diagonals, only then run beads in a skip pattern, cool slowly, re-check with a straightedge — [Texas Metal Works](https://texasmetalworks.com/product/50x80-welding-table-top)
- Tack first, then straighten members that moved (with heat) before final welding; "the real trick is clean fitup of the frame and keeping it dead flat when welding it" — [WTAT forum (15622)](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=15622); [Garage Journal (180145)](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/this-could-be-the-ultimate-welding-table.180145/post-6126707)
- Japanese practice: use a steel plate as a stand-in surface plate (定盤) with welding magnets, clamp, then tack — [WELDTOOL (weldtool.jp)](https://www.weldtool.jp/article/yousetsu-mokuteki/4977); alternate left/right and top/bottom welds to spread heat — [DIYレシピ](https://diy-recipe.com/recipe/8642/); a hobbyist reports distortion "always" appeared and squareness was hard, so he stopped at tack welds only — [ameblo blog](https://ameblo.jp/monstera0909/entry-12380530181.html); weld the longitudinal direction first to limit bow when joining square tube into a rectangle — [maniacucina](https://maniacucina.work/diy/kakupaipeweld/)
- Taiwan Fineworks build: magnetic squares + tack welds to fix the base tube before full welding; adjustable feet "in case the floor is uneven, and it makes levelling the top easier" — [小間製作所 Fineworks.tw](http://www.fineworks.tw/modules/xforum/viewtopic.php?post_id=498)

### Inferences
- A practical sequence for this user (synthesised from the above): (1) buy the top first and check it with a straightedge/string before accepting it; (2) support the top face-down on three or four levelled stands/beams and shim until a straightedge and diagonal strings show it flat; (3) build the top frame (rails + cross-members) directly on the back of the plate, clamped to it, tacking everything before welding, welding only frame-to-frame joints in an alternating order; (4) add legs and stretchers with the assembly still inverted, checking diagonals; (5) flip, fit adjustable feet, level, then shim/bolt or lightly stitch the top.
- Crossed diagonal strings (two strings corner to corner; they just touch in the middle if there is no twist) and winding sticks made from two straight lengths of new rectangular tube are free tools that reveal twist better than a short level.
- New rectangular tube faces are themselves a reasonable straightedge ("pretty darn flat on the faces" per Practical Machinist), so two long tubes on stands, shimmed co-planar, make a usable temporary build surface — the same idea as "welding rails".

### Gaps
- The WeldingWeb thread was only seen through search excerpts; reply-by-reply detail (who recommended what) is not captured.
- No excerpt explicitly described the crossed-string method; it is included as my inference.

## 5. Surface care — mill scale, spatter, rust in humid climates, paint, grounding

### Takeaway
Most hobby builders leave mill scale on (it holds an oil/wax film and slows rust) and clean to bright metal only where the work/earth clamp makes contact; rust control is a routine wipe with paste wax, WD-40 or Boeshield T-9 — the sources offer nothing specific to a humid sub-tropical shop beyond "do it more often".

### Cited Findings
- Leave it: mill scale acts as "a kind of protective oxidation" and has "tooth" that better holds a film of oil; downside is it transfers grime to work and gloves — [Welding Tips and Tricks forum, "remove mill scale on welding table top?"](https://www.forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=13257)
- Remove it (at least locally): "mill scale does not ground well" — a TIG user got arc strikes and pits in the table top; scale, rust and spatter between table and part cause arcing — [WeldingWeb, "Mill Scale on Welding Table"](https://www.weldingweb.com/threads/557561); [WeldingWeb, "Mill scale? New table should it stay or go?"](https://www.weldingweb.com/threads/718571)
- AWS forum thread quotes AWS D1.5 as permitting mill scale that withstands vigorous wire brushing, a thin rust-inhibitive coating or anti-spatter compound to remain; a welder there says consistency "goes way up" when scale is removed — [AWS forum](https://app.aws.org/forum/topic_show.pl?tid=12649) (this is about weldments, not table tops)
- Rust prevention used by forum members: Minwax paste wax ("the yellow can"); WD-40 sprayed about every two weeks and wiped; Boeshield T-9 wiped weekly; a user with a heated/cooled low-humidity shop reports no rust at all — [Miller forum, "No rust welding table top?"](https://forum.millerwelds.com/forum/welding-discussions/14105-no-rust-welding-table-top); [Garage Journal, "How to finish steel welding table?"](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/how-to-finish-steel-welding-table-removing-mill-scale-years-of-hard-rust-etc.314373/)
- Avoid galvanized (zinc) surfaces because of fumes; a light film of oil or wax on a bare steel top reduces spatter adhesion — [coohom guide](https://www.coohom.com/article/diy-welding-tables-build-your-custom-workspace-today) (low-quality content site; consistent with general practice)
- If casters are welded on, wrap the bearings to keep spatter out — [TractorByNet (392188)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-10)
- Jody Collier suggests an aluminium top/cover when working on stainless or polished parts — [weldingtipsandtricks.com](https://www.weldingtipsandtricks.com/welding-table.html)
- The Instructables "Professional Grade" table's bill of materials includes paint (for the frame/legs) — [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/DIY-Professional-Grade-Welding-Table-with-FREE-Pla/)

### Inferences
- For Taiwan's humidity: leave the scale on the general surface, flap-disc a bright strip along one edge/corner (or weld on a dedicated earth lug/stud under the top) for the return clamp, paint everything except the top face and the reference edges, and keep a wax or light-oil film on the top; expect to re-wipe weekly in the plum-rain/typhoon season. Cold-rolled or ground bars (as used in several slat builds) have no scale and will flash-rust much faster — they need the film from day one.
- Paste wax is preferable to oil where parts will later be painted (less contamination of weld joints); any film must be kept off the joint area because oil causes MIG porosity.
- With CO2 MIG (more spatter than mixed gas), anti-spatter spray on the top around the weld zone and a scraper/flat file for nubs is more practical than any attempt to keep the top pristine.

### Gaps
- No source found that addresses welding-table rust prevention specifically in tropical/humid climates.
- Nothing retrieved on copper or aluminium backing bars/plates as table accessories, nor on anti-spatter products specifically for table tops.
- No sourced discussion of dedicated grounding studs found; the grounding points above come from mill-scale threads.

## 6. Top edge details, squaring fences, stops and jigs for batch work

### Takeaway
Builders consistently want 50–150 mm of clear overhang for clamps and two truly square reference edges; for repeat production of identical frames the proven pattern is removable stops set once against a square corner (pins in holes, or bolted stops), with a dedicated fixed jig only when the run is long.

### Cited Findings
- Overhang: ~6 in (150 mm) all round recommended; 2–3 in (50–75 mm) commonly built; a frame too close to the edge makes clamping harder — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3654731); [TractorByNet (392188)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-10)
- A design with 30 × 60 in (0.76 × 1.52 m) × 3/8 in top used a 2–2.5 in (50–64 mm) overhang on all sides — [TractorByNet, "Welding Table Build" (183165)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.183165/post-2090521) (attribution approximate)
- Square edges enable repeat work: "Having the sides square allow the stops to be dropped to pull the frame out, then repositioned without measuring to build the next one" — [Practical Machinist, "Welding clamps that fit in hole in table"](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/welding-clamps-that-fit-in-hole-in-table-hole-specs.398618/)
- "Once you set your stops for the first corner of a frame, you can drop every subsequent piece into the exact same spot" — [The Jimbo Slice Workshop, Fireball table write-up](https://thejimbosliceworkshop.com/fireball-welding-table/)
- Repeat jobs on a modular table: "I make the jig for it, and take a few digital photos. If it repeats, I can set the jig back up in 2–3 minutes from the photo" — [Practical Machinist (398618)](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/welding-clamps-that-fit-in-hole-in-table-hole-specs.398618/) (attribution approximate)
- DIY removable stops: drill and tap a grid of 1/4-20 (≈M6) holes in a jig base; stops with jam nuts lock in place "creating custom configurations in seconds" — [weldhive.com](https://weldhive.com/how-to-make-a-welding-jig/)
- Fixed dedicated jigs make sense "when you're running hundreds of identical parts"; rotate weld positions on production jigs to spread heat — [Kaijian vendor guide](https://www.kjweldingtable.com/welding-table-jigs-how-to-choose-and-use-them-for-precise-metalwork)
- Fireball's argument that a 2 in hole grid beats tee-slots for accurate, repeatable fixture placement — [Fireball Forum](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/avoid-these-mistakes-when-you-build-a-fixture-welding-table/2902)
- Extras seen on builds: receiver hitch tube under the top for vise/accessory mounts (TractorByNet 353032); recessed lower shelf so tools don't fall off when rolling, swing-out seat and bins (DTN) — [TractorByNet (353032)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.353032/); [DTN/Progressive Farmer](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/equipment/article/2021/10/17/diy-welding-table-portable-takes)

### Inferences
- For batch stands from square tube without a full fixture grid: (1) bolt a straight length of 50 × 50 tube or 10 × 50 flat bar along one long edge as a fence, squared to (2) a second bolt-on fence across one end, giving a permanent 90° corner; (3) make drop-in/bolt-on stops (short flat-bar blocks with a slot and one bolt) that locate the other members; (4) on a slatted top the gaps themselves take T-bolts or clamp bars so stops can be positioned anywhere along a gap; on a plate top a row of tapped M10/M12 holes or a few 16 mm holes at 100 mm pitch near the fences is enough. Fences must be bolted (removable), never welded to the top.
- Overhang must not be so large on a thin top that the edge deflects under hammering/clamping; ~75–100 mm on a 10–12 mm top is a reasonable compromise between the quoted 50 mm and 150 mm.
- Rounded/chamfered top corners and a scribed/stick-on rule along the fence edge are common-sense details; they were not explicitly documented in retrieved sources.

### Gaps
- Nothing retrieved specifically on rounded corners, scribed rulers/tape on the table edge, or purpose-built squaring fences on DIY plate tables — these remain unsourced.

## 7. Commonly cited mistakes and regrets

### Takeaway
The recurring regrets are about stiffness and heat, not strength: tops too thin for their span, tops welded too much, long tables without centre support, bowed plate accepted at purchase, too little clamp overhang, clamp gaps/holes laid out badly, and casters/height not planned together.

### Cited Findings
- Top too thin: thin sheet "will warp and droop with the slightest addition of heat"; 1/4 in tops oil-can; "1/2 minimum as I have experienced issues"; 3/8 in on a 4 × 8 ft span judged "not very stout" — [coohom guide](https://www.coohom.com/article/diy-weld-table-your-essential-guide-to-building-a-sturdy-work-surface); [metalworkinghub](https://metalworkinghub.com/how-to-make-a-welding-table/); [IH8MUD](https://forum.ih8mud.com/threads/welding-table.824324/page-2); [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3654731)
- Starting with a warped plate (it cannot realistically be straightened and it transfers to every job) — [Fireball Forum](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/avoid-these-mistakes-when-you-build-a-fixture-welding-table/2902)
- Welding legs straight onto the top / seam-welding the top / over-welding from underneath → permanent bow, dimples — [Everlast forum](https://www.everlastgenerators.com/node/869); [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3660325); [Extreme How-To](https://extremehowto.com/how-to-build-a-welding-table/)
- Welding a finished (imperfect) frame to the plate pulls the plate to the frame's shape — [WTAT forum (15622)](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=15622)
- No centre legs on a long table: 1/8 in (3 mm) sag over 10 ft (3.05 m) developed over time — [Practical Machinist](https://www.practicalmachinist.com/forum/threads/standard-duty-welding-table-critique-my-plan.302352/)
- Frame too close to the edge / too little overhang for clamps — [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/post-3654731)
- Slat gaps too narrow (25 mm) for clamps; drilling slat mounting holes before fitting slats to the frame — [Pelican Parts forum](https://forums.pelicanparts.com/porsche-911-technical-forum/1029323-building-welding-table.html); [TractorByNet (386590)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.386590/)
- Fixture-hole mistakes: 3/4/6 in hole pitches that fit no standard tooling; sloppy hole location ("1/16 is close enough"); too many holes weakening the plate and removing heat-sink mass — [Fireball Forum](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/avoid-these-mistakes-when-you-build-a-fixture-welding-table/2902); [Fireball Forum (2643)](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/diy-fixture-table/2643); [HomemadeTools forum](https://www.homemadetools.net/forum/welding-table-95112-post217970)
- Fixed, non-adjustable height — [Fireball Forum](https://fireball.discourse.group/t/avoid-these-mistakes-when-you-build-a-fixture-welding-table/2902); "set the height to whatever is comfortable for you, not what others suggest" — [Pro-Touring](https://www.pro-touring.com/threads/94427-Welding-table-construction-advice-needed)
- Casters: undersized for a heavy plate top; forgot that they add ≥76 mm to height; one owner would rework his to lower the table 3–4 in (75–100 mm) and move the casters further apart front-to-back for stability; put locks at the ends where they can be reached; make them liftable so the table sits on its legs in use — [TractorByNet (392188)](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-10); [TractorByNet, "Crazy idea for a welding table?"](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/goto/post?id=4367128); [TractorByNet (302426)](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/)
- Trying to straighten after the fact: "everything I try to straighten usually ends up worse than it was before I started" — [TractorByNet welding-table threads](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.302426/page-4) (exact post not confirmed)
- Trusting a table to stay flat forever: a welded 9.5 mm top drifted out of flat in its most-used area after years — [WTAT forum (14342)](https://forum.weldingtipsandtricks.com/viewtopic.php?t=14342)
- Weight under-estimated: a ~100 kg table needs a helper just to manipulate it during welding — [Instructables](https://www.instructables.com/DIY-Professional-Grade-Welding-Table-with-FREE-Pla/); 9 mm plate "is very heavy" for a hobbyist — one Japanese answer suggests 6 mm steel over plywood instead — [Yahoo!知恵袋](https://detail.chiebukuro.yahoo.co.jp/qa/question_detail/q11252037071)

### Inferences
- Mistakes from the task brief that the retrieved excerpts did **not** document (so treat as unverified folk wisdom): "drawers full of grit", "table too big to move", "table not level". The closest sourced items are the DTN recessed shelf (to stop things falling off) and the adjustable-feet advice.
- Almost every regret is avoidable at the design stage by: buying a flat top and checking it at the yard; frame first; top bolted/shimmed; 6 legs or deep rails beyond ~1.8 m; ≥75 mm overhang; 45–50 mm slat gaps; casters sized for ≥2× total weight and combined with jack-down feet.

- Storage/drawer regrets and "too big to move" regrets were not found in retrievable sources.

### Inferences
- The builds nearest this user's brief (≈0.75–0.9 × 1.8–2.4 m, heavy but cheap, chop saw + grinder only) are #3 (slat table on 50 × 100 rectangular tube) and #1/#2 (plain thick plate on a ladder frame). Both are achievable without laser cutting. The Taiwan build #14 shows local laser-cutting of a 10 mm, 16 mm/50 mm grid top is practical at small size (1000 × 700), suggesting a hybrid: a plain or slatted main table plus a small laser-cut fixture plate/section for jig work.
- Build #15 (5 mm top on 50 × 50 × 3) represents the light end of what Taiwanese hobbyists build; by the forum evidence in sections 2 and 7 a 5 mm top is too thin for a "重載" table used with a 250 A MIG.
- US steel prices in builds #1–#2 are undated and not transferable to Taiwan; they are useful only as ratios (the plate is roughly half to two-thirds of total material cost).
