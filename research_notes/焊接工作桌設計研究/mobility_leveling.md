> Method note for the report writer: in this session every direct page fetch (Garage Journal, TractorByNet, Hobby-Machinist, WeldingWeb, Fireball Tool, Instructables, BangShift, trick-tools) was blocked by the network allowlist. All findings below come from search-engine excerpts of those pages. Quotes are as excerpted; where one search returned several threads from the same forum, the thread attribution is the best match and may be off by one thread. Nothing was verified against full page text, photos or video. Shopee (蝦皮) / Ruten (露天) listings did not surface in any search, so NT$ prices are from RS Taiwan and momo only.

## 1. What mechanisms do builders and manufacturers use to combine casters and leveling feet? (a–f, pros/cons, cost, failures)

### Takeaway
The dominant DIY answer is exactly what the user describes: ordinary casters stay fixed, and a separate screw foot (bolt through a welded nut) beside each caster is wound down 2–3 mm past wheel level to take the weight. Integrated leveling casters (Footmaster type), retractable/cam casters and trailer jacks all exist but each has a documented weakness for this use: tiny wheels and only ~15 mm of adjustment, limited lifting mechanism strength, or wobble.

### Inferences
- Option (a) is the only one that matches the user's stated requirement (wheels stay wheels, six independent screw-down points) and it is also the cheapest and the most frequently reported as successful. Options (b) and (c) solve "get it off the wheels" but not "level a 2.4 m table on a bad floor", because adjustment range is about 15 mm and the wheels are 50–75 mm.
- Footmaster-type casters would make sense only as the four rolling casters (they are compact and rated 500 kg+), but then their built-in foot duplicates the separate jacks; plain 100–125 mm casters roll far better on a workshop floor with slag and grinding debris.
- Trailer jacks give long travel and a crank from above, but forum reports of wobble plus their bulk (retracted length) make them a poor fit for six points; they are better suited to one-end "wheelbarrow" designs.
- Purchased machine leveling mounts from RS at NT$760–2,600 each would cost roughly NT$4,600–15,600 for six; a hardware-store bolt, nut and a fabricated pad is a fraction of that and is what most forum builders used.

### Gaps
- No Shopee/Ruten/hardware-store (五金行) NT$ prices were found for Footmaster casters, clones, or 調整腳; RS Taiwan is an industrial distributor and probably the upper bound on price.
- No Chinese-language (Mobile01, PTT) DIY welding-table thread on casters plus leveling feet surfaced.
- Carrymaster leveling-caster (AC series) ratings were not found; only the non-leveling ACM-600F / ACMT-600F at 1,764 lb each appeared ([Access Casters](https://accesscasters.com/acmt-600f-carrymaster-low-height-high-capacity-twin-wheel-swivel-caster)).
- No documented structural failure (bent screw, stripped nut, collapsed caster) with numbers was found; failure reports are qualitative.

## 2. Screw-type leveling feet: thread size, capacity, fabrication, cranking, foot pad, travel

### Takeaway
Forum builders overwhelmingly use 5/8"–1" bolts (≈M16–M24), most often 3/4" (≈M20), through a nut welded to the leg, a plate or a side bracket. Even an M16 class 4.6 rod has a proof load of about 35 kN, so strength is not the limiting factor for a 150–400 kg table; thread engagement length, shock loading, wear and ease of turning are.

### Inferences
- Strength check (my calculation from the cited proof loads): a 400 kg table plus a 300 kg workpiece is about 6.9 kN total. If the whole load landed on a single foot, an M16 class 4.6 screw (35.3 kN proof) still has a factor of about 5, M20 about 8, M24 about 11.5. In normal use on six feet each sees roughly 1.1–2.3 kN. Any of M16, M20 or M24 is strong enough in pure compression.
- The real selection criteria are therefore (1) bending/buckling of the exposed screw when the table is shoved sideways or hammered on, (2) thread engagement length, (3) availability of matching nuts, coupling nuts and sockets, and (4) turns needed. M20 (pitch 2.5 mm) is the best balance and matches the forum-favourite 3/4": 2.5 mm per turn means about 12 turns for 30 mm. M24 (3 mm per turn) is stiffer and slightly faster but heavier and costlier; M16 (2 mm per turn) is adequate for a 150 kg table but is the slender choice at 400 kg with 50 mm of screw exposed.
- Engagement: a single standard nut is only about 0.8×D tall (the forum's "3/4 nut is about 3/4 tall" point). A long coupling nut (長螺帽 / 接頭螺帽, typically about 3×D) or two nuts welded in line roughly triples engagement, keeps the screw straighter and reduces wobble. This is the direct fix suggested on TractorByNet.
- Cranking from above (the "outrigger" feel): put the nut low on a side bracket or inside a short square-tube sleeve welded to the outside of the leg, run a long threaded rod up through it, and weld or double-nut a hex on the top end so a socket on a ratchet, a speed handle or an impact driver can turn it from standing height. With M20 full nuts the drive hex is 30 mm across flats (M16: 24 mm; M24: 36 mm; standard ISO sizes, not sourced here). Using an ordinary hex-head bolt upside-down gives the same result with the head as the drive hex if the bolt is long enough.
- Coarse versus fine: one builder regretted coarse thread, but for a table that must be jacked 20–40 mm every time it is parked, coarse M20 (2.5 mm) keeps the turn count tolerable and coarse rod and nuts are what Taiwanese hardware stores stock. An impact driver or cordless ratchet removes the effort argument for fine thread.
- Foot pad: the screw tip should not bear directly on concrete (it walks and gouges). A swivel pad can be bought (調整腳 with ball joint, 6–30° tilt) or fabricated: a 60–80 mm disc of 6–10 mm plate with a short ring or drilled cup that captures the rounded rod end, or simply a fender washer welded to the bolt head as on Garage Journal (rigid, fine on a reasonably flat floor). A rigid welded pad forces the pad to rotate on the floor as the screw turns; a captured ball/cup pad does not.
- Travel budget (my estimate): foot must retract at least 10–15 mm above the wheel contact line for rolling clearance over floor joints, must extend 2–3 mm below wheel level to unload the casters, plus the floor's out-of-level across the footprint (allow 15–25 mm over 2.4 m in a typical workshop), plus margin. That gives about 40–60 mm of usable travel, so a 150–200 mm long M20 bolt or rod per point is appropriate once nut length and pad thickness are added. This is roughly double the 15 mm that Footmaster-type casters offer and matches the 40–50 mm that Siegmund and Strong Hand provide.
- Keep the threaded parts shielded from spatter (a bolt left in the nut during nearby welding, or anti-spatter spray), given the conflicting forum advice; never run the welding current through the screw thread.

### Gaps
- No source gives a tested lateral (bending) capacity for a leveling screw at a given exposed length; the stiffness argument above is engineering judgment, not a cited figure.
- No source found for nut thread-stripping capacity versus engagement length (ISO 898-2/-6 tables were not legible).

## 3. Is 6 leveling points good practice? 3 vs 4 vs 6 supports, twist/sag over 1.8–2.4 m, and the leveling procedure

### Takeaway
Six supports are normal commercial practice for tables longer than about 1.2 m and do help a long top stay flat, but more than three supports is statically indeterminate: the four corners must define the plane first and the middle pair must only be snugged to contact, otherwise the feet themselves bow or twist the top.

### Cited Findings
- A welding-table retailer's answer to "why are there 6 legs on the 3×5 table": "Any length over 4 foot gets an extra support leg to help keep the table flat and true." — [Trick-Tools answers](https://answers.trick-tools.com/4374167/Why-is-there-6-legs-on-the-3x5-welding-table)
- Siegmund adjustable-height caster legs are sold as a set of six for its larger tables. — [Trick-Tools: Adjustable Height Caster Legs for Siegmund Welding Table (Qty 6)](https://www.trick-tools.com/Adjustable_Height_Caster_Legs_for_Siegmund_Welding_Table_Qty_6_S1-280861_5586)
- Three non-collinear points define a unique plane, so three supports cannot rock; a fourth contact point "almost certainly doesn't fall on the same plane that the first three legs have already created", which is why four-legged tables wobble (over-constraint). — [Core77: Helpful Design Constraints vs Over Constraint](https://www.core77.com/posts/67317/Helpful-Design-Constraints-vs-Over-Constraint)
- Patent literature states the same: four or more equal rigid legs constrain the feet to a plane, so they "are unable to all contact the ground simultaneously when the surface is uneven". — [USPTO 8607715 Stabilisation of objects](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8607715)
- WeldingWeb: leveling a table fully takes about three adjustment points, while curing wobble takes only one. — [WeldingWeb: Threaded Leveling Legs for Welding Table](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/717163-Threaded-Leveling-Legs-for-Welding-Table)
- Six-leg leveling sequence (billiard-table mechanics, the closest documented six-leg procedure): "the trick to leveling a 6 leg table, is to first level the frame square using the 4 corner legs"; another poster in the same thread says "start in the middle work out from there". — [AZBilliards: Leveling a 6 legged table](https://forums.azbilliards.com/threads/leveling-a-6-legged-table.117958)
- Staged leveling: get side-to-side level first and, once it stops changing, move to end-to-end, checking with a longer straightedge or level. — [Epilog: Table leveling procedure](https://support.epiloglaser.com/laser-machine/legend-36ext/calibrations/table-leveling-procedure-legend-36ext/)
- A table maker argues leg count matters less than structure: a table succeeds or fails on how much the whole structure bends under real load and how much it twists when load lands off-centre, and torsional resistance comes from the rib grid. — [Texas Metal Works: How thick should a welding table be](https://texasmetalworks.com/2026/08/03/how-thick-should-a-welding-table-be-wrong-question-heres-what-matters/)
- "A table can leave the factory with a perfect 0.004″ flatness, get strapped to a pallet, torqued by a forklift, and show up at your shop with 0.020″ of twist. Then it gets set on a concrete floor that isn't level, and the spec is meaningless." — [Texas Metal Works FAQ: How flat should a welding table be](https://texasmetalworks.com/faq/how-flat-should-a-welding-table-be-and-what-tolerance-is-acceptable)
- Sag between cross ribs rises with rib spacing to the fourth power (maker's teaching model, explicitly "not a stamped analysis"). — [Texas Metal Works: Welding table deflection calculator](https://texasmetalworks.com/2026/08/06/welding-table-deflection-calculator-see-what-actually-bends/)
- A hobbyist's four-corner-leg frame racked out of square during welding, leaving one leg about 1/2" (≈13 mm) off the floor; advice was to tack corners first to lock geometry, then finish welding, shimming a leg to correct. — [TractorByNet: Basic Welding Fab Ques](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/goto/post?id=4101118)
- Adjustable-height legs "often reduce the load capacity of the table but do give the table more versatility". — [GlobalSpec: Welding Tables and Fixturing Tables](https://www.globalspec.com/learnmore/manufacturing_process_equipment/manufacturing_equipment_components/welding_tables_fixturing_tables)

### Inferences
- Six points (front/back × left/middle/right) is consistent with commercial practice at 1.8–2.4 m and is a sound choice, provided the user treats it as "4 corners define the plane, 2 middle feet remove sag". Because deflection of a beam scales with span to the third or fourth power, halving the unsupported span from 2.4 m to 1.2 m with a middle pair cuts self-weight and load sag of the long rails by roughly an order of magnitude. That is general beam theory, consistent with the retailer's "over 4 ft gets an extra leg" rule.
- The risk of six jack screws is that each one is a 2.5 mm-per-turn press. An over-tightened middle foot lifts the centre and crowns the top; an over-tightened corner twists it. A 400 kg table with a tube frame will visibly follow its feet.
- Suggested procedure (synthesised from the cited sources):
  1. Roll into position; wind all six feet down until they just touch.
  2. Raise the two middle feet clear again. Jack on the four corners only until all casters are 2–3 mm off the floor.
  3. Level across the width at one end (two corner feet), then at the other end, with a machinist's or good spirit level on the top; then level along the length.
  4. Check for twist: level across the width at both ends and the middle; if the two ends disagree the top is twisted, so correct with one corner foot. A string pulled corner-to-corner on both diagonals over equal spacers, or winding sticks, shows twist without needing the floor to be level.
  5. Lay a long straightedge or tight string along each long edge; bring each middle foot down until it just removes the sag (string gap equal at the middle and the ends). Stop at firm contact; do not lift.
  6. Rock the table and re-check; no jam nuts are needed according to the Garage Journal report, but they are cheap insurance for hammering work.
- A three-point alternative (two feet at one end, one at the other) cannot twist the top, but it gives a triangular footprint that is bad for tip-over on a 75–90 cm wide table with a vise or chop saw at a corner; six points with a disciplined sequence is the better trade.
- Whether the middle legs "help" depends on frame stiffness: if the long rails are, for example, 50×100 mm tube under the top, sag over 2.4 m is already small and the middle feet mainly add stiffness against hammering in the centre.

### Gaps
- No controlled test comparing 3-, 4- and 6-leg welding tables for twist or flatness was found; the retailer's statement is a rationale, not a measurement.
- No manufacturer's written six-leg welding-table leveling sequence was retrieved (the BuildPro manual excerpt did not show the sequence); the procedure above is synthesised and partly inferred.
- No deflection numbers specific to the user's planned top thickness and rail sections were found; a calculation needs those inputs (covered by other researchers).

## 4. Caster selection: diameter, material, brakes, load-rating math, layout and mounting position

### Takeaway
Size each caster for total weight ÷ 3 plus 20–50% margin, pick steel/cast-iron or polyurethane-on-iron wheels rather than rubber (slag burns rubber), and mount them where they allow a bigger wheel and do not steal the leg position from the jack foot. Because the jacks carry the working load, the casters only need to carry the table in transit.

### Cited Findings
- Load rule: required rating = W/(C−1), i.e. assume one caster lifts off on an uneven floor; the "3-corner rule" is loaded weight ÷ 3 plus 20% (worked example 800 lb → 320 lb → choose 350 lb casters). ICWM minimum margin is 20%, and "most serious industrial OEMs size at 30–50% above". — [CasterHQ: Caster load capacity calculator & weight rating guide](https://casterhq.com/blogs/news/caster-load-capacity-calculator-weight-rating-guide)
- Caster Concepts recommends an additional 33% capacity, noting carts on uneven floors "may have only three casters on the ground at any given time". — [Caster Concepts: Why is the listed weight capacity different](https://casterconcepts.com/blog/casters/why-is-the-listed-weight-capacity-different-than-what-youre-telling-me)
- Alternative method on the same vendor's site: a four-caster cart on an uneven floor can put 40–50% of the load on the heaviest caster; apply a 1.3–1.5× distribution factor before the safety factor. — [CasterHQ: Safety factor](https://casterhq.com/blogs/engineering-specifications/safety-factor)
- Overload behaviour: at 2× catalogue rating caster components begin to deform; at 4× expect catastrophic failure. — [Caster Concepts: What is a safety factor for casters](https://configurator.casterconcepts.com/blog/safety-casters-and-wheels/what-is-a-safety-factor-sf-for-casters-how-do-i-improve-ours-and-other-important-safety-related-questions/)
- Wheel material for welding: "I prefer steel wheels for the weld table. Rubber doesn't like weld slag." — [Garage Journal: Welding Table; Casters or Leveling Feet? (post 8204959)](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/welding-table-casters-or-leveling-feet.436992/post-8204959)
- TractorByNet makes the same point: sparks and slag burn rubber-faced wheels; double-lock (total-lock) swivel casters are recommended and described as "very stable". — [TractorByNet: Welding Table Casters](https://tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-casters.263377)
- Ratings at the same size are similar across materials: 6" casters at 1,250 lb cast iron, 1,200 lb phenolic, 1,200 lb polyurethane-on-iron. — [W.T. Hight Product Bulletin](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0110/6074/0158/files/W.T._Hight_Product_Bulletin_05.11.16R.pdf)
- Commercial welding-table casters: Uline sells 6"×2" phenolic swivel-with-brake casters for its mobile welded steel tables; a McMaster welding cart uses phenolic wheels rated 1,500 lb; Fireball's Dragon Wagon caster is a 5" wheel rated 750 lb on an M30×3.5 stem (Pro series: 1/2-13 stem, 350 lb). — [Uline H-4998SWB](https://www.uline.com/Product/Detail/H-4998SWB/Steel-Workbenches/Phenolic-Caster-for-Mobile-Welded-Steel-Tables-Swivel-with-Brake-6-x-2); [McMaster welding carts](https://www.mcmaster.com/products/welding-carts/); [Fireball replacement casters](https://www.fireballtool.com/products/heavy-duty-caster-for-dragon-wagon-legs)
- Siegmund caster legs: 200 kg per caster (Professional 750, 30 mm height adjustment) and 150 kg per caster (Basic); the heavy-duty caster leg is 1,500 kg static per leg but 600 kg when rolling at 4 km/h. Siegmund notes specified capacities are static. — [Siegmund Professional 750](https://siegmund.com/en/1-Welding-Tables/122-Professional-750/Overview,102.php); [Siegmund Basic 16](https://siegmund.com/en/1-Welding-Tables/70-Basic/Overview,102.php)
- Undersizing warnings: casters rated about 300 lb each were called too small for a heavy table; cheap wheels wear flat spots. — [TractorByNet: Building a Shop / Weld Table](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/page-4); [WeldingWeb: Casters on welding table](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/498471-Casters-on-welding-table)
- A roughly 3×5 ft table weighing about 700 lb (≈320 kg) loaded rolls easily on 3" plate casters. — [TractorByNet: Welding Table Build](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.183165/post-2092634)
- Position: a caster directly under the leg flexes less than a wheel mounted off to the side. — [WeldingWeb: Castors for Welding Table](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/714049-Castors-for-Welding-Table)

- A DIY plan uses two swivel casters and two swivel casters with brake plus 6" steel feet. — [Heavy Duty Welding Table (Instructables mirror)](https://structables.lunar.icu/Heavy-Duty-Welding-Table)

- Taiwan supply: HOCASTER 5" series: rubber wheel with cast-iron frame rated 400 kg; PU wheel with aluminium frame rated 450 kg; available as swivel, braked swivel (rear double brake) and rigid. — [Misumi Taiwan catalogue page (HOCASTER)](https://tw.c.misumi-ec.com/book/OC21_TW_01/pdf/0022.pdf)
- Taiwan retail price points: momo lists a 3" PU plate swivel caster claimed at 250–300 kg for NT$75–150 and a 4" TPR braked swivel rated 70 kg for NT$130. RS Taiwan lists a LAG 150 mm fixed castor, 450 kg, at NT$2,358 ex-tax. — [momo TP00004960000011](https://www.momoshop.com.tw/TP/TP0000496/goodsDetail/TP00004960000011); [momo TP00012560000206](https://www.momoshop.com.tw/TP/TP0001256/goodsDetail/TP00012560000206); [RS Taiwan LAG 4352759](https://twcn.rs-online.com/web/p/castor-wheels/4352759)

### Inferences
- Rating math for this table (my calculation with the cited rules): heaviest case 400 kg table plus the load carried while rolling (say 100 kg) = 500 kg; ÷3 = 167 kg; +20–50% = 200–250 kg per caster. For the 150 kg light case, 100 kg casters suffice. A set of four casters rated at least 250 kg each covers the whole range. Workpiece weight during welding does not count if the table is on its jacks.
- Four casters at the corners are simpler than six: six rigid-mounted casters on an uneven floor still share load unpredictably and the middle pair makes the table pivot. Keep six jacks but four casters.
- Wheel diameter: 100–125 mm (4–5") is the practical choice. Larger wheels roll over slag, joints and cables far better than the 50–75 mm wheels of leveling casters, but every extra millimetre of caster height is height the jack screw must also span. A 125 mm caster has an overall height of roughly 150–165 mm, so the jack assembly needs that reach plus travel.
- Material: polyurethane on cast-iron or steel core (鐵芯PU輪) is the usual compromise (quiet, floor-friendly, tolerates sparks far better than rubber/TPR); all-cast-iron or steel wheels are the most slag-proof but noisy and hard on painted or epoxy floors. Phenolic is what US commercial welding tables ship with; I found no Taiwanese retail source for it.
- Brakes: total-lock (雙剎 / wheel and swivel both locked) on at least two casters is still worth having for the moments before the jacks are down and on sloped floors, even though the jacks do the real holding.
- Swivel/rigid: four swivels give the best manoeuvrability in a tight workshop and the "walks sideways" objection disappears once all corners are jacked; two rigid plus two swivel tracks straighter over long distances. For a 2.4 m table in a small shop, four swivels (two with total lock) is the more useful layout.
- Position: with a jack foot at every leg, mounting the casters on the lower cross rails or stretchers slightly inboard of the legs keeps the jack screws at the outermost footprint (best tip-over stability when parked) and lets the screws run alongside the leg where they can be reached from above. Caster inset should be kept small (50–100 mm) to preserve rolling stability.
- Prices on momo for "250–300 kg" 3" casters at NT$75–150 are implausibly cheap for the claimed rating; treat marketplace ratings sceptically and buy from a caster brand with a published datasheet.

### Gaps
- No source addressed heat/slag resistance of polyurethane versus phenolic versus cast iron with data; only forum preference for steel over rubber.
- No Taiwanese NT$ price was found for a 5" or 6" heavy-duty PU-on-iron total-lock caster; needs a Shopee/五金行 check.
- The Misumi catalogue excerpt named the HOCASTER maker inconsistently in search output; the 400/450 kg figures should be confirmed on the catalogue page.

## 6. Stability: does lifting off the casters matter, and tip-over with a chop saw and long stock

### Takeaway
Forum experience says locked casters still have perceptible play, so resting on solid feet matters for hammering, vise work and keeping a levelled reference, although several owners of heavy tables find total-lock casters alone acceptable. No source quantified tip-over, but the consistent advice is a wide footprint, support for overhanging stock, and pushing the table lengthwise.

### Cited Findings
- "There's enough play in the mechanism and/or the point contact with the ground that there's still noticeable movement from the work surface" even with locking casters. — [Garage Journal: Stability of locking/leveling casters](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/stability-of-locking-leveling-casters.552835/)
- Locking casters "will likely have some play", so a truly stable platform should rest on its base; all casters should lock or there should be a way to take the weight off them with leveling feet. — [Garage Journal: Best locking swivel casters with no play?](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/best-locking-swivel-casters-with-no-play.395670/)
- Counter-view: "the total locking casters are more stable than I had anticipated, so out with the feet", and "the weight of a stout welding table will keep it in one place". — [Garage Journal: Welding Table; Casters or Leveling Feet?](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/welding-table-casters-or-leveling-feet.436992/)
- Counter-view the other way: one member holds that a welding table should not move at all and keeps a separate mobile station; a builder who skipped locks wished he had chosen locking casters. — [Garage Journal: Welding Table; Casters or Leveling Feet?](https://www.garagejournal.com/forum/threads/welding-table-casters-or-leveling-feet.436992/)
- Some welders skip wheels entirely or use temporary wheels or a pallet jack, because casters get in the way when bending parts in the vise. — [WeldingWeb: How do you weld on casters?](https://weldingweb.com/vbb/threads/637841-How-do-you-weld-on-casters)
- "A level table makes many welding setups much easier." — [TractorByNet: Welding Table Build](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/welding-table-build.183165/post-2090521)
- Moving a heavy table: pushing or pulling lengthwise "will be TWICE as stable as moving the table SIDEWAYS"; keep the vise low when moving. — [TractorByNet: Building a Shop / Weld Table](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/goto/post?id=5056407)
- A builder questioned whether a 2" overhang all round, leaving the end legs 26" outside-to-outside, would make the table tippy; the thread gave no definitive answer. — [TractorByNet: Building a Shop / Weld Table](https://www.tractorbynet.com/forums/threads/building-a-shop-weld-table.392188/post-5056634)
- Saw-stand warning: "once a heavy workpiece is cut, the centre of gravity may be displaced, causing the saw stand to tip over". — [Bosch GCM working advice](https://bosch-professional.com/manuals/professional/be/nl/online-manual/200419635/en-GB/19538172279140957323.html)
- A narrow support "will tip over the second a heavy piece of steel hangs off the edge"; supports for long stock must be level with each other or the metal binds the blade. — [JourneymanHQ: Metal saw horses for supporting long bars](https://www.journeymanhq.com/?p=521531)
- Long stock overhanging a saw table can tip or shift; use outfeed support that carries the load without changing workpiece position. — [Woodsmith: Secret for cutting long stock](https://www.woodsmith.com/article/secret-for-cutting-long-stock/)

### Inferences
- For accuracy, lifting off the wheels matters less for the weld itself than for (1) keeping the top in the plane it was levelled to, since casters creep and swivel, and (2) dead-solid feel when hammering, prying in a vise or grinding. With a 150 kg table the difference is large; with 400 kg it is smaller but still there because of swivel play.
- Tip-over is best in the parked state if the six jack feet are at the outer edges of the 75–90 cm width. The vulnerable state is rolling: casters inset from the edge narrow the track, and a vise or chop saw mounted at a corner raises and offsets the centre of gravity. Roll lengthwise, with nothing overhanging.
- Chop-saw overhang check (my estimate, not sourced): a 6 m length of light tube or angle weighs roughly 15–30 kg; with half of it overhanging one end, the overturning moment is far below the restoring moment of a 150–400 kg table that is 1.8–2.4 m long, so end-wise tipping is not a concern. Side-wise overhang across the 75–90 cm width with heavy sections is the case to respect; a roller stand at saw-bed height solves both tipping and blade binding.
- Putting the chop saw at one end so stock feeds along the table's length uses the table's long axis for stability and lets the top act as the infeed support.
